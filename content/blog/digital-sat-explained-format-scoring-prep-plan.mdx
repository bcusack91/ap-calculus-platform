The SAT looks very different than it did a few years ago. It's now fully digital, noticeably shorter, and — most importantly — **adaptive**. If you learned about the SAT from an older sibling or an out-of-date prep book, some of what you "know" no longer applies. Here's a clear, current breakdown of how the exam actually works and how to prepare for it efficiently.

## The Big Picture

The Digital SAT is taken on a computer using College Board's **Bluebook** application, either on your own device or one provided at the test center. Compared to the old paper test, it is:

- **Shorter** — about 2 hours and 14 minutes instead of three hours.
- **Adaptive** — the test adjusts to your performance as you go.
- **Calculator-allowed for all math** — and there's a built-in Desmos graphing calculator on screen.
- **Built around shorter reading passages** — each reading question has its own brief passage rather than one long passage with many questions.

The score scale is unchanged: **400 to 1600**, made of two sections scored 200–800 each.

## How the Adaptive Format Works

This is the part students most often misunderstand. Each section — Reading & Writing, and Math — is split into **two modules**.

1. You take the **first module**, which has a mix of easy, medium, and hard questions.
2. Your performance on that first module determines whether your **second module** is an easier set or a harder set.
3. Doing well on module one routes you into the harder module two, which is what unlocks the top of the score range.

A practical consequence: **the first module matters a lot.** You can't see future questions or go back to a previous module, so it pays to be sharp and accurate early. Within a single module, though, you *can* move freely between questions, flag ones to revisit, and change answers.

## Section 1: Reading and Writing

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Modules | 2 |
| Questions | 27 per module (54 total) |
| Time | 32 minutes per module |
| Passage style | One short passage (or pair) per question |

Reading & Writing blends what used to be separate Reading and Writing/Language sections. Questions fall into four broad categories:

- **Craft and Structure** — vocabulary in context, purpose, and how texts connect.
- **Information and Ideas** — main ideas, details, inference, and command of evidence (including questions based on charts and graphs).
- **Standard English Conventions** — grammar, punctuation, sentence structure.
- **Expression of Ideas** — rhetoric, transitions, and meeting a stated writing goal.

Because each passage is short, the skill being tested is **precision**, not stamina. You're rewarded for reading carefully and choosing the answer the text actually supports — not the one that sounds impressive.

## Section 2: Math

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Modules | 2 |
| Questions | 22 per module (44 total) |
| Time | 35 minutes per module |
| Calculator | Allowed on all questions |

About 75% of questions are multiple choice and 25% are **student-produced responses** ("grid-ins") where you type your own answer. The content breaks down into four areas:

- **Algebra** — linear equations, systems, and inequalities.
- **Advanced Math** — quadratics, exponentials, polynomials, and functions.
- **Problem-Solving and Data Analysis** — ratios, percentages, probability, and interpreting data.
- **Geometry and Trigonometry** — area and volume, angles, triangles, and the unit circle.

Even though a calculator is always allowed, the students who score highest don't reach for it on every problem. Knowing your core algebra cold is faster than typing everything into Desmos — but Desmos is a genuine advantage for graphing, solving messy systems, and checking work.

## How the Digital SAT Is Scored

Your raw performance in each section is converted to a scaled 200–800 score. Because the test is adaptive, the second module you were routed into is factored into the conversion — reaching the harder module is what makes the highest scores attainable. There is **no penalty for wrong answers**, which leads to the single most important strategic rule:

> **Never leave a question blank.** A guess has a chance of being right; a blank never does.

## A Practical 6-Week Prep Plan

Six weeks is enough time to make a meaningful jump if you practice deliberately. Here's a structure that works:

**Week 1 — Diagnose.** Take one full official practice test in Bluebook under realistic conditions. Don't study first; you want an honest baseline. Then categorize every miss by topic and by *type* (content gap vs. careless error vs. timing).

**Week 2 — Attack your weakest math area.** Pick the math category where you lost the most points and drill it specifically. Learn the concept, then do 20–30 targeted problems and review every miss.

**Week 3 — Attack your weakest Reading & Writing area.** Do the same for verbal. If grammar (Standard English Conventions) is weak, that's the fastest area to improve because the rules are finite and learnable.

**Week 4 — Build timing.** Start doing modules under the real clock. The goal is to finish with a few minutes to spare so you can revisit flagged questions. Practice the habit of flagging and moving on instead of getting stuck.

**Week 5 — Full-length practice.** Take another complete official test. Compare your error log to week 1 — your old weak spots should be shrinking, which tells you the targeted practice is working.

**Week 6 — Polish and rest.** Light review of your remaining weak topics, one more timed module or two, and then taper. Sleep matters more than cramming the night before.

## Test-Day Logistics That Trip People Up

- **Install and update Bluebook** ahead of time and complete the "exam setup" the app prompts you to do a few days before.
- **Charge your device fully** and bring your charger; know your school's policy on loaner devices.
- **Learn the on-screen tools early** — the Desmos calculator, the answer flagger, the highlighter, and the countdown timer. Fumbling with these on test day wastes time you can't spare.

## The Bottom Line

The Digital SAT rewards the same things the old one did — strong fundamentals and careful reading — but it adds two new strategic realities: the **first module is pivotal**, and the **on-screen tools are part of the test**. Build your core skills, practice in Bluebook so the interface feels automatic, never leave a question blank, and treat every practice test as data rather than a verdict. Do that consistently, and the score will follow.
