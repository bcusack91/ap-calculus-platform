You have **one week** until the AP US History exam. This plan covers all 9 periods systematically, alternates between content and FRQ skill-building, and includes a full mock exam on day 6. Aim for 4–5 hours per day.

## Your week at a glance

| Day | Focus | Time | Key Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 (Mon)** | Periods 1–3 (colonial → Revolution) | 4 hrs | Timeline, doc recognition, causation notes |
| **2 (Tue)** | Period 4 (Early Republic → Jacksonian) | 4.5 hrs | Expansion, regional conflict, document drills |
| **3 (Wed)** | Period 5 (sectional crisis → Reconstruction) | 4.5 hrs | Slavery debate, Civil War causes, 14th Amendment context |
| **4 (Thu)** | Periods 6–7 (Gilded Age → New Deal) | 5 hrs | Industrialization, progressive reform, Great Depression causation |
| **5 (Fri)** | Periods 8–9 (Cold War → Present) + DBQ workshop | 5 hrs | Containment, Civil Rights, modern polarization + analyze 1 practice DBQ |
| **6 (Sat)** | Full mock exam (MCQ + FRQ) | 3.5 hrs | Timed under exam conditions; score + review |
| **7 (Sun)** | Weaknesses + night-before reset | 3 hrs | Revisit problem areas, sleep, mental prep |

## Daily structure template

Each day follows this rhythm to maximize retention:

1. **Timeline review (20 min):** Place major events in order. Don't just memorize dates — understand *why* they happened and what came after.
2. **Document + context (40 min):** Read 3–4 key primary sources. For each, note author, audience, purpose, point of view (HIPP).
3. **Causation writing (30 min):** Write one quick thesis on "**Why did X cause Y?**" Builds the muscle memory.
4. **Comparison framing (30 min):** Write one thesis on "**How did X and Y compare?**" Forces nuance.
5. **Practice problems (90 min):** MCQs from that period, at least 1 FRQ excerpt.
6. **Synthesis (20 min):** Jot down 2–3 "big ideas" that connect this period to later ones.

## Day 1 (Monday): Periods 1–3 — Colonial foundation through Revolutionary upheaval

**Timeline anchors:**
- 1491: Pre-Columbian Americas
- 1607: Jamestown (Virginia Company)
- 1638: Harvard founded
- 1692: Salem witch trials
- 1707: Act of Union (Scotland + England)
- 1754–63: French & Indian War
- 1765: Stamp Act & resistance
- 1775: Lexington & Concord
- 1776: Declaration of Independence
- 1781: Yorktown; Articles ratified
- 1787: Constitutional Convention

**Key documents (Period 1–2):**
- Columbus letters (purpose: justify voyage)
- Mayflower Compact (consensus covenant model)
- Bacon's Rebellion account (class + race tensions)
- Great Awakening sermon (emotional vs intellectual religion)

**Key documents (Period 3):**
- Stamp Act Congress resolutions (no taxation without representation)
- Jefferson's *Summary View of the Rights* (natural rights theory)
- Federalist No. 10 (faction concern)
- Anti-Federalist Papers (local power > federal)

> 💡 **Causation chain you'll see on the exam:** Mercantilist restrictions → colonial resentment → taxes without representation → armed resistance → independence rationale. Get it cold.

**Writing practice:** "Evaluate the extent to which the American Revolution was ideological versus economic in motivation."

## Day 2 (Tuesday): Period 4 — Early Republic through Jacksonian democracy

**Timeline anchors:**
- 1789: Washington's presidency; Hamilton's financial system
- 1800: Jefferson election (peaceful transfer of power)
- 1803: Louisiana Purchase
- 1807–09: Embargo Act
- 1812–15: War of 1812 ("Second American Revolution" narrative)
- 1820: Missouri Compromise
- 1828: Jackson election; "spoils system"
- 1830: Indian Removal Act; Trail of Tears
- 1832: Nullification Crisis
- 1836: Gag Rule (slavery debate silenced in Congress)

**Causation focus:** **Why did the West matter?** Westward expansion → land hunger → conflict with Native Americans → slavery extension debate → regional sectionalism.

**Key documents:**
- Hamilton's *Report on Manufactures* (federal economic role)
- Jefferson's *First Inaugural* ("wise and frugal government")
- Monroe Doctrine (US hemispheric influence)
- Calhoun's *South Carolina Exposition* (nullification doctrine)
- Jackson's bank veto message (executive power vs Congress)

> ⚠️ **Trap:** Jackson is often portrayed as "common man" president, but he was a slaveholder who removed thousands of Native Americans. Balance the "democratic" narrative.

**Writing practice:** "Compare the extent to which Jefferson and Jackson changed executive power."

## Day 3 (Wednesday): Period 5 — Sectional crisis and the Civil War

**Timeline anchors:**
- 1844: Polk election; Manifest Destiny
- 1845: Texas annexed
- 1846–48: Mexican-American War; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo
- 1850: Compromise of 1850
- 1852: *Uncle Tom's Cabin* published
- 1854: Kansas-Nebraska Act; bleeding Kansas
- 1857: Dred Scott v. Sandford
- 1859: John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry
- 1860: Lincoln elected; Confederate secession
- 1861–65: Civil War
- 1865–77: Reconstruction; 13th, 14th, 15th Amendments

**Causation focus:** **Why did slavery become THE sectional issue?** Westward expansion made slavery's future in new territories the battleground. Free vs. slave state balance = political power.

**Comparison focus:** **How did Union and Confederate views of federalism differ?** Union (Lincoln): states cannot secede; federal authority supreme. Confederacy: sovereign states can nullify federal law.

**Key documents:**
- Lincoln's *House Divided* speech (incompatibility thesis)
- Secession declarations (state sovereignty claims)
- Emancipation Proclamation (war aim shift)
- 13th Amendment (slavery abolished)
- Freedmen's Bureau records (Reconstruction conditions)

> 🎯 **Causation question you'll see:** "Did the Civil War cause Reconstruction or did earlier failures cause both?" Answer: all three (territorial expansion, political deadlock, incompatible economies).

**Writing practice:** "Evaluate the extent to which the failure of compromise between 1850 and 1860 made Civil War inevitable."

## Day 4 (Thursday): Periods 6–7 — Industrialization through the New Deal

**Timeline anchors (Period 6):**
- 1869: Transcontinental RR completed
- 1879: Edison light bulb
- 1886: Haymarket Affair; AFL founded
- 1892: Homestead Strike
- 1896: Bryan "Cross of Gold"; McKinley elected

**Timeline anchors (Period 7):**
- 1901: Theodore Roosevelt presidency
- 1906: *The Jungle* published; meat inspection
- 1913: Federal Reserve established
- 1917: US enters WWI
- 1920: 19th Amendment (women's suffrage)
- 1921–29: Harding/Coolidge prosperity
- 1929: stock crash → Great Depression
- 1932: FDR elected
- 1933: New Deal "100 Days"
- 1935: Social Security Act; Wagner Act

**Continuity focus:** **Capitalism evolved but inequality persisted.** 1890s: monopolies and labor exploitation → Progressive reforms limit trusts → 1920s: corporate consolidation resumes → 1929: unregulated stock market crashes.

**Comparison focus:** **How did Progressive Era and New Deal approaches to federal power differ?** Progressives: regulate bad actors (monopolies, corrupt politicians). New Deal: direct government intervention (jobs programs, social insurance).

**Key documents:**
- Muckraker excerpts (*The Jungle*, Ida Tarbell oil trust exposé)
- TR's "Square Deal" message (labor mediation, conservation)
- Woodrow Wilson's war message (1917; democracy promotion)
- FDR's fireside chats (government as guarantor)
- Supreme Court strikes down early New Deal laws (federalism clash)

> ⚠️ **Trap:** New Deal *didn't* end the Great Depression — WWII military spending did. Know the limits of reform.

**Writing practice:** "To what extent did the New Deal represent a change from or continuation of Progressive Era policies?"

## Day 5 (Friday): Periods 8–9 & DBQ workshop

**Morning: Periods 8–9 (Cold War through present)**

| Period | Themes | FRQ weight |
|---|---|---|
| **8 (1945–1980)** | Containment, proxy wars, civil rights, backlash, Vietnam | ~25% |
| **9 (1980–present)** | Reagan conservatism, end of Cold War, 9/11, globalization, polarization | ~15% |

**Key causation chains:**
- Wartime alliance (US-USSR) → postwar tension → Truman Doctrine → NATO → Korean War containment strategy
- Jim Crow South + northern discrimination → Brown v. Board → Civil Rights movement → 1964–65 legislation → white backlash → modern inequality continues
- Vietnam escalation → anti-war movement → generational divide → Ford/Carter foreign policy caution → Reagan revival of Cold War rhetoric

**Afternoon: DBQ workshop (2 hrs)**

1. **Read a practice DBQ** from the CED ([sample DBQ on Reconstruction governance](/courses/ap-us-history/cram/frq-practice)).
2. **Categorize the 7 documents:** Which ones support thesis A? B? What's missing? Which author has suspicious bias?
3. **Write your thesis:** Use the prompt's verb (evaluate extent, assess, compare). Plant your specific argument upfront.
4. **Draft one body paragraph:** Topic sentence (mini-thesis) + evidence from 2–3 docs (cite them!) + analysis of *why* they matter.
5. **Score yourself** against the rubric: thesis, contextualization, evidence, reasoning.

## Day 6 (Saturday): Full mock exam (3.5 hrs, timed)

**Simulated exam:**
- 55 multiple-choice (80 minutes)
- 1 DBQ (60 minutes)
- 1 LEQ (40 minutes)

Do this under timed, quiet conditions. No notes, no phone. **Score it immediately after.**

> 🎯 **Diagnostic:** If you score under 60% on MCQs, drill another day of Periods 3–5 (they're weighted highest). If your FRQ thesis is missing a specific argument, revisit the [FRQ practice guide](/courses/ap-us-history/cram/frq-practice).

- Revisit your mock exam weak areas (30 min).

- Review the top 20 must-know terms on that page.
